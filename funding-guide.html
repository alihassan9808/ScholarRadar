How to evaluate a funded scholarship offer

A tuition waiver is not necessarily a fully funded offer. Use this guide to compare the money you actually receive, the costs you must cover, and the conditions attached to funding. It is designed for international master's and doctoral applicants.

1. Calculate your monthly funding gap

Use one currency for every amount. Enter monthly equivalents, including annual expenses divided by 12. Leave uncertain costs blank until you obtain a written estimate; the calculator treats blanks as zero, so an incomplete estimate can be misleading.

Enter your own estimates to calculate.

Example: a stipend of 900 and costs of 350 rent, 250 food and transport, 50 insurance, 100 fees and 100 other expenses leaves 50 per month. A separate deposit or flight may still require upfront savings. These numbers are illustrative, not a cost-of-living estimate for any country.

2. Compare the written funding terms

CheckWhat to ask the provider
TuitionIs the waiver complete, and are registration or laboratory fees excluded?
StipendWhat is the net amount, payment schedule and first payment date?
DurationDoes funding cover the expected degree length, including thesis submission?
RenewalWhat progress, grades or teaching obligations are required?
HousingIs accommodation guaranteed, subsidized or entirely self-funded?
TravelAre flights, visa costs and relocation reimbursed, and when?

Compare offers over the same period. A twelve-month stipend cannot be compared directly with a nine-month stipend without accounting for the unfunded months. Do not count a possible teaching assistantship as guaranteed income unless the offer confirms it.

3. Build an application evidence file

Before applying, save the official call, eligibility rules and deadline with its time zone. Record the exact program and scholarship names separately: admission and funding may require different applications. For research degrees, identify whether supervisor consent is required or merely helpful. A professor's informal expression of interest is not a scholarship award.

For each document, record its issuer, validity date, translation requirements and whether the university requires a certified copy. Request references early and give referees the program name, deadline and a concise summary of your research experience. Keep a submission receipt and check whether the portal requires a separate scholarship form.

4. Verify before accepting

Use the institution's official admissions or funding office to resolve discrepancies. If the offer and public scholarship page differ, ask for written clarification. Check visa financial requirements with the relevant immigration authority; a scholarship letter may not cover every required expense.

Useful official starting points: DAAD, Chevening, Study in Japan, and EducationUSA. These are provider or government resources, not endorsements or guarantees of eligibility.

Editorial note: this is an independent planning guide, not a funding offer. Calculator inputs stay on this page and are not submitted to ScholarRadar.
